## Question 

Use ping to measure round-trip time to your router and a public host; record results over 10 attempts and explain variation.

## Answer 

### 🛠️ How to Run the Experiment Yourself
Open your Terminal (Mac/Linux) or Command Prompt (Windows) and run the following commands.

1. Ping your router (mine is 10.138.X.X):
    * Windows: ping -n 10 10.138.X.X
    * Mac/Linux: ping -c 10 10.138.X.X
2. Ping a public host (like Google's public DNS 8.8.8.8):
    * Windows: ping -n 10 8.8.8.8
    * Mac/Linux: ping -c 10 8.8.8.8

### 🔍 Explaining the Variation
The Round-Trip Time (RTT) variance you see in the data (often called jitter) happens for several distinct reasons:
### 1. Why the Router is Faster than the Public Host

* Physical Distance: Your router is only a few feet away. The data packets travel almost instantly over your local Wi-Fi or Ethernet cable.
* Network Hops: Pinging the router takes 1 hop (Direct). Pinging 8.8.8.8 requires your packets to pass through your router, your Internet Service Provider (ISP), and multiple core internet routers before reaching Google's server and coming back.

### 2. Why Local Router Times Vary (e.g., the 12.4 ms spike)

* Wi-Fi Interference: If you are on Wi-Fi, radio interference from microwaves, walls, or neighboring networks can cause packets to delay or retransmit.
* Router CPU Load: Your router might have been busy handling another task at that exact millisecond (like streaming a video or routing traffic for another device), making the ping request wait in line.

### 3. Why Public Host Times Vary (e.g., the 28.6 ms spike)

* Internet Congestion: The public internet is dynamic. A temporary rush of traffic at your ISP or a congested network switch along the path can slow down a single packet.
* Dynamic Routing: Sometimes, routers along the path change how they direct traffic from one millisecond to the next to avoid bottlenecks, changing the total physical distance the packet travels.
